### Seasonality (tourism effect)

Strongly seasonal, and the seasonality is tourism-driven rather than resident-driven.

- Foreign tourist arrivals to Portugal hit a monthly record of **2.3 million in August 2024**, up 5.3% YoY; 13.1 million arrivals in the first eight months of 2024; tourism-linked consumption ~**€44bn, 16.5% of GDP** (Tier 1/2 — Reuters citing INE, the national statistics institute; article dated 30 Sep 2024, checked 26 Jul 2026).
- Lisbon short-term-rental demand peaks in **September, August, July** and troughs in **January, February, December**; peak-month occupancy 68.6% vs low-month 37.9% — a ~1.8× swing (Tier 3, AirROI, checked 26 Jul 2026).
- Crowd intensity ratings for Lisbon: "Very High" Jul–Aug, "High" Jun and Sep, "Low" Jan–Feb (Tier 3, WhenVerdict, checked 26 Jul 2026).

**Implication that matters for pricing:** NomNom's demand curve will be materially higher Jun–Sep. But that peak is composed disproportionately of tourists, who are the *wrong* audience for a subscription (they need one city, once) and the *right* audience for a one-time snapshot purchase. This is the strongest single argument in this document for prioritising Snapshot over Subscription — see Recommended Pricing.

### Geographic hotspots within Portugal

TheFork's 50 most-booked restaurants in Portugal in 2025: **42 of 50 are in Greater Lisbon**; the remainder are split across Greater Porto, Aveiro and Coimbra (Tier 2 — Lisboa Secreta and Marketeer/SAPO both reporting TheFork's published ranking; checked 26 Jul 2026). Top three: Cervejaria Ramiro (Lisbon), Garden Balthazar Caffe (Lisbon), Contrabando (Almada).

This is direct, quantified support for NomNom's Lisbon-first / Porto-second sequencing. Note the 42/50 concentration is *more* extreme than a population split would predict, so Porto may be a materially weaker second market than assumed.

### Rising related queries

- Portugal-specific: `perto de mim` ("near me") and `aberto agora` ("open now") reported as growing **4.5× in Google Search and >6× in Google Maps on mobile** (Tier 3, vendemkt.com, 26 Jul 2026 — no methodology or time window given; treat as directional only).
- Category-level 2025–2026 trend, corroborated across three independent sources: discovery is shifting from **aggregate star ratings to named-person curation**. Time Out Lisboa's 2026 list explicitly frames the market around curated editorial; Lisboa Secreta's 2026 outlook states "o público procura, cada vez mais, experiências com identidade" (the public increasingly seeks experiences with identity); Food Network and Demand Curve both attribute Beli's growth to replacing anonymous reviews with trusted personal ones. This is a genuine tailwind for NomNom's *product* thesis (creator curation) even though, as shown below, it is not yet a tailwind for its *monetisation* thesis (consumers paying for that curation).

### Post-mortem evidence — Cream (Tier 2, founder's own written post-mortem, Medium)

Cream was a restaurant discovery app built on **expert/chef curation** — structurally the closest failed analogue to NomNom that exists in the public record. It ran 24 months and shut down. The founder's stated reasons, verbatim in substance:

1. They had built *"a product that users wouldn't pay for (and so requiring scale to sell their attention to others)."*
2. **"EGC [expert-generated content] doesn't generate the exponential amount of fast, free traffic & virality that UGC does"** — because fewer people generate content, so the revenue model (selling attention) cannot reach viability before runway ends. **This is a direct, specific warning about NomNom's creator-curation supply model.**
3. Market sizing: ~9M Australians in the target audience, but Zomato Australia — the category leader with a similar audience — had only 2M users across Melbourne and Sydney combined, across both web and mobile. The founder's conclusion: *"these 9M were potential users — not customers."*
4. *"Helping people discover the best food & drink isn't an acute enough problem."*

Corroborating failure patterns from adjacent post-mortems: Blok24 (Tier 2, GeekWire) shut down primarily on **retention** — restaurant and bar content could not lift one-week retention, described as "a content issue and secondarily a value proposition versus what Google and Facebook were doing."

## Willingness to Pay — consumer side

### Evidence FOR

1. **Three direct competitors sustain paid consumer tiers.** Mapstr (€59/yr), World of Mouth (€46.80–€118.80/yr) and Truffle ($29.99/yr) all operate live consumer paywalls in this exact category. Mapstr in particular has enough paying users that its pricing change generated sustained public complaint — you only get an angry mob if you also have a crowd. (Tier 1)
2. **Mapstr's gate design is validated by its own backlash.** The 300-place limit is described by Mapstr as "several years' use for most of our users" (Tier 1, own FAQ) — meaning the paywall bites only the heaviest users. That is a well-targeted gate.
3. **Europe pays a premium and prices are rising.** +29–39% vs North America; +18% YoY. (Tier 2, Adapty)
4. **A named one-time-purchase demand exists and is loud.** See the Snapshot evidence in the next section.
5. Portuguese consumers already tolerate €9–13/month price points for Spotify, Netflix and YouTube Premium. (Tier 3, geopriced)

### Evidence AGAINST

1. **The most structurally similar product that ever tried this, failed, and the founder's diagnosis was explicitly about willingness to pay.** Cream: *"a product that users wouldn't pay for."* And: *"Helping people discover the best food & drink isn't an acute enough problem."* (Tier 2)
2. **Every breakout product in the category is free.** Beli — the one product with genuine cultural momentum, 75–80M ratings and 4.27★ — is free, and grew via referral, not purchase. Seekeasy MCP: free. Menu, please! (220 PH points): free. Crispy List: no visible paywall. The market's revealed preference is unambiguous.
3. **Beli's own commentators treat monetisation as an unsolved problem, not a solved one.** Demand Curve, writing about Beli: *"How will they monetize? Will they charge everyone a monthly...?"* — open question. A Substack analyst scored Beli **3/5 on product-market fit**, noting *"the market size may be limited to only foodies and (low) dining frequency."* (Tier 3)
4. **Mapstr's paywall produced real, quotable churn to free substitutes.** Verbatim App Store reviews: *"60€ per year for such a modest feature is absolute insanity… 1 or 2€ per month would be understandable, but 5€ per month is a cash grab"*; *"the price asked is not worth the service provided"*; *"I've since switched to Google map."* Multiple reviewers name **Pin Drop** as a free substitute they moved to. (Tier 3, App Store reviews via MWM aggregator)
5. **The free substitute is excellent and universal.** Google Maps lists are free, already installed, and already contain the user's saved places. Blok24's post-mortem named exactly this: *"a content issue and secondarily a value proposition versus what Google and Facebook were doing."* (Tier 2)
6. **Only one verified consumer revenue figure exists in indie food discovery and it is $325/month from 24 subscribers.** (Tier 2, RevenueCat-verified)
7. **Lifestyle is a winner-take-almost-all category:** the top 10% of Lifestyle apps take 97.9% of revenue. The median Lifestyle app earns close to nothing. (Tier 2, Adapty)

### Verdict

**Consumers will pay for restaurant-discovery apps, but only narrowly, only at low price points, and only for a utility they cannot easily replicate for free — and the price ceiling is lower than the brief assumed.**

The functional benchmark is Mapstr: at €59/year it retains paying users but generates visible, articulate churn to free alternatives, with several reviewers independently naming **€1–2/month** as the price they *would* have accepted. That is a striking convergence: the market is telling Mapstr, unprompted, that its price is roughly 2.5–5× too high for the perceived value.

The paid tiers that survive in this category gate a *storage/utility* function (unlimited saves, unlimited maps), not a *content* function. NomNom's plan to keep unlimited personal saves free is therefore strategically clean — no Mapstr-style backlash — but it also means NomNom has **deliberately given away the only thing in this category that has been proven to convert to payment.** That is a real and material risk, and it should be stated as such in the financial model rather than glossed.

## Willingness to Pay — creator-sold lists specifically

This is NomNom's core bet. I searched it four different ways. Here is everything I found, for and against.

### Precedent 1 — Beli Paid Guides (strongest evidence FOR; Tier 1)

Beli — the category's breakout consumer app — **operates creator-sold guide subscriptions today.** Its published subscriber terms establish: creators set their own price; billing recurs at creator-designated intervals; Beli bills on the creator's behalf via App Store or card; creators may change pricing at any time; subscriptions auto-renew.

This is close to an exact match for NomNom's "Subscription" tier, running live, at the most credible company in the category. **NomNom's core bet is not unprecedented — the market leader has already placed it.**

Two heavy caveats. First, **Beli publishes no price points and no adoption data**, so I cannot tell you whether Paid Guides is a success, a pilot, or a quiet failure. `DATA GAP: Beli Paid Guide actual price points, number of participating creators, number of paying subscribers, and whether the feature is still being promoted. This is the single highest-value unknown in this entire document and is partially resolvable by installing Beli and inspecting live creator profiles.` Second, the fact that Beli built this rather than a straight consumer subscription is itself informative: it suggests Beli concluded its users would not pay Beli directly, but might pay a *person*.

### Precedent 2 — Rexby: creators sell city guides, and one-time "lifetime" pricing is the live pattern (Tier 1)

Rexby is a venture-funded ($4M seed) platform whose entire premise is creators selling curated place recommendations. A live creator storefront shows **€59 for lifetime full access to the map and all itineraries, including all future additions** — with locked and unlocked recommendations visible side by side, exactly NomNom's free/paid list split.

**This is a working, priced, real-world implementation of NomNom's "Snapshot" concept — except it is sold as *lifetime including updates* rather than as a frozen point-in-time snapshot.** That distinction matters commercially: the creator explicitly markets *"when you buy access, I keep adding new places whenever I find new"* as the reason to buy. A snapshot frozen at purchase date is a strictly *worse* offer than what this market has already established, and NomNom should expect price resistance for it.

Rexby's own blog claims top guides earn **$5,000–$15,000/month** (Tier 1 source but promotional and self-interested; "some of the top guides" is survivorship-selected and should not be modelled).

### Precedent 3 — creator guide platforms converge on a price band (Tier 2)

Two independent creator-guide platforms publish pricing guidance:
- **Spotli.st:** $5–8 (5–10 spots) · $9–15 (15–25 spots) · $16–25 (30+ spots) · $25+ (expert)
- **Tourli:** "we recommend $9–29"

These overlap tightly. For a city food guide of roughly 15–30 spots, the established market band is **$9–25 / roughly €8–23** as a one-time purchase.

### DISCONFIRMING EVIDENCE — and it is serious

**1. The most candid assessment of this market comes from an analyst writing *about* Rexby, and it is damning (Tier 3, Substack):**

> *"Currently, there is not much of a paying tradition for online travel guides… Due to the sheer volume of information online available to everyone, powered by search engines, algorithms, and community engagement, it is more of an expectation rather than a fact for people to pay for online travel guides, which have a plethora of substitute products."*

The same analyst identifies the *only* condition under which the premium is justified: **information asymmetry.** *"I am unlikely to buy an online travel guide from an ordinary person… but if the person happens to be an extremely professional local, then it might save me time and energy."* Note that even this framing describes a market that still needs to be *created* — the analyst's argument is that paying for guides *could become* normal at scale, not that it is normal now.

**This applies with extra force to NomNom.** Rexby sells to *travellers* who face genuine information asymmetry in a foreign country. NomNom's stated primary target is **urban Portuguese diners aged 24–34 in their own city** — the group with the *least* information asymmetry and the largest stock of free substitutes (their own friends, their own Instagram feed, Time Out Lisboa's free 100-restaurant list, TheFork, Google Maps). The precedent that works best is the one that maps least well onto NomNom's stated core user.

**2. Successful food creators monetise via restaurants, not via their audience.** This is the clearest disconfirming pattern I found, and it is consistent across every source. Chicago Food Guide — a genuinely successful local food-guide media business with a queue of 20 restaurants waiting to be posted about — earns from **restaurants paying for content and influencer-marketing brokerage.** It does not sell lists to its audience. The published food-creator rate cards are all restaurant-funded: nano $30–$350/post, micro $150–$2,500, macro $1,000–$20,000; food creator CPMs $12–$35. (Tier 2/3, multiple independent sources)

The revealed preference of the entire food-creator economy is that **the restaurant pays, not the diner.** That is a direct commercial validation of NomNom's *third* revenue line — marked sponsored placements — and a direct challenge to its first two.

**3. Cream's expert-generated-content warning.** Cream curated chef and expert recommendations and concluded EGC cannot generate enough volume or virality to reach viability. NomNom's creator-list model has the same supply-side shape.

**4. I found zero evidence of anyone paying for a restaurant list *in their own city*.** Every priced precedent — Rexby, Spotli.st, Tourli, WoM's travel framing — is travel-oriented. `DATA GAP: any documented instance of consumers purchasing curated restaurant lists for the city they live in. I searched for this specifically and found none. This is an absence of evidence, not proof of absence, but the absence is conspicuous given how much travel-guide evidence surfaced.`

### Honest verdict

**The bet is live, not proven — and the strongest precedents are for tourists, not locals.**

What is genuinely established: creators can sell curated place guides; the established price band is €8–23 one-time; the format that sells is *lifetime access including updates*; and Beli has already built creator-sold subscriptions inside a restaurant app.

What is not established, and what NomNom is actually betting on: that a **resident** will pay a **recurring monthly fee** for **restaurant lists in the city they already live in**. I found no precedent for that specific transaction. Every working example substitutes at least one of those three variables — traveller instead of resident, one-time instead of recurring, or trip-planning instead of dining-out.

**Ranking NomNom's three revenue lines by strength of external evidence, best to worst:**

1. **Sponsored restaurant placements** — strongly supported. The entire food-creator economy already runs on restaurants paying, with published rate cards, and Portugal's restaurant density plus TheFork's Lisbon concentration means the buyer base exists locally.
2. **Snapshot (one-time list purchase)** — moderately supported. Priced precedents exist (Rexby €59 lifetime; platform guidance €8–23), and there is documented, unmet, *vocal* demand for one-time purchase in this exact category — Mapstr users writing *"If there was a one time payment, I would do that in a heartbeat and I would be willing to pay a decent amount if it was a one-time fee."* No competitor offers it. **This is NomNom's clearest differentiated wedge.** Caveat: sell it as lifetime-with-updates, not a frozen snapshot.
3. **Creator subscriptions** — weakest. One live precedent (Beli) with zero published outcome data, and the analyst consensus is that no paying tradition exists for recurring curated-recommendation access. Build it, but do not let the financial model lean on it.

## Recommended pricing range for NomNom

All figures EUR, Portugal market, as of 26 July 2026.

### 1. Snapshot — one-time list purchase → **€3.99 – €7.99**, with a €9.99 ceiling for flagship lists

**Reasoning:**
- The creator-guide platform band ($5–25 / €5–23) is the outer envelope, but that band is set by *travel* guides bought during high-intent trip planning, where the buyer is already spending €800+ on the trip. A local buying a Lisbon list has a far lower reference price.
- €3.99–€7.99 sits in genuine impulse territory and is defensible against the free substitutes (Time Out's free 100-restaurant Lisbon list, Google Maps lists) that a Portuguese resident already has.
- It undercuts a Mapstr annual (€59) by ~10× and a WoM annual (€46.80) by ~8×, making "buy one list" an obviously low-risk first transaction.
- Mapstr's own churned users volunteered that they'd pay *"a decent amount"* one-time. Nobody quantified "decent," so I will not invent one — but the willingness is documented and currently unserved by any competitor.
- Portugal indexes *below* the DACH/Nordics/UK/France high-WTP cluster, so the top of the creator-guide band is not available here.
- **Sell it as lifetime access including the creator's future updates**, matching the Rexby pattern, not as a frozen point-in-time file. The evidence says updates are the stated reason people buy.

### 2. Creator subscription — **€2.99 – €3.99/month**, or **€24.99 – €29.99/year**

**Reasoning:**
- €3.90/month is exactly where World of Mouth prices its annual-billed tier — the closest editorial-curation comparable in Europe. Pricing at or just under it is defensible.
- Mapstr's effective €4.92/month (€59/12) is empirically **above** the tolerance line for this category in Europe: it produced quotable churn and users naming €1–2/month as acceptable. €2.99–€3.99 sits between the market's stated preference (€1–2) and the surviving competitor's price (€4.92).
- Annual median across 75,000+ apps is $29.99 and the most-common annual price is also $29.99 (RevenueCat) — so €24.99–€29.99/year is squarely at market, not a discount.
- Annual subscribers retain at **44.1%** at 12 months vs **17.5%** monthly, and European monthly subscribers are the most price-sensitive segment at renewal. **Push annual hard.**
- Avoid weekly pricing. It retains at 3.4% at 12 months and would read as predatory in a trust-based creator product.

### 3. Sponsored restaurant placements — **`DATA GAP`, but the anchors are known**

I will not invent a placement price. The relevant anchors: Portuguese/European micro-influencer food posts run **$150–$1,200** ($200–$800 typical for restaurant deals); nano **$30–$350**; food CPMs **$12–$35 per 1,000 impressions**; restaurants typically allocate **3–10% of marketing spend** to influencer activity, which for a €3,000/month independent is **€90–€300/month**. Visito.me successfully charges Portuguese-adjacent European restaurants **€69/month** for a SaaS product, which is a useful read on restaurant software budgets. `DATA GAP: Lisbon-specific restaurant marketing budgets and prevailing local sponsored-content rates. Requires primary research — call 10 Lisbon restaurants.`

### 4. Structural recommendation

**Lead with Snapshot, not Subscription.** Three converging reasons: (a) it is NomNom's only genuinely differentiated position — no verified competitor offers one-time purchase, and there is documented unserved demand for it; (b) demand is tourism-seasonal and peaks Jun–Sep with visitors who will never subscribe but might buy once; (c) it is the lower-trust transaction, so it will convert first while the creator base is still being built.

## Data Gaps

Ordered by how much damage each one does to the financial model if left unfilled.

1. **`Beli Paid Guides: actual price points, creator count, subscriber count, and whether the feature is still active.`** The single most valuable unknown in this document. It is the only Tier 1 precedent for NomNom's exact core bet, and its outcome is invisible from outside. **Partially resolvable in 30 minutes** by installing Beli and inspecting live creator profiles for prices and subscriber counts.
2. **`Any documented instance of a consumer paying for a curated restaurant list in their own city.`** Searched four ways; found none. Every priced precedent is travel/tourist-context. This is the load-bearing assumption in NomNom's model and it is currently unevidenced.
3. **`Google Trends direction and index values for "onde comer lisboa", "melhores restaurantes lisboa", "restaurantes lisboa", "Beli app" (geo=PT, 5yr).`** Not retrievable via web search. A 15-minute manual task. Without it, "search demand" cannot honestly be scored above Moderate.
4. **`Portuguese median disposable income for the urban 24–34 cohort.`** Needed to convert European sticker-price benchmarks into a defensible Portuguese affordability ceiling. All my price recommendations currently rest on competitor comparables rather than on local ability to pay.
5. **`Lisbon-specific restaurant marketing budgets and prevailing sponsored-content rates.`** The sponsored-placement line is the best-evidenced revenue stream and I have no local price for it. Requires primary research — 10 phone calls.
6. **`Savor, Crumble and Nomblr — could not be located as consumer restaurant apps.`** Searches surfaced only unrelated products (Crumbl cookies, Crumble POS, Truffle POS). Either these are pre-launch, defunct, regionally invisible, or misnamed in the brief. Names should be verified before further research is commissioned.
7. **`Crispy List install base, pricing, monetisation.`** The closest currently-shipping analogue to NomNom's concept, and entirely opaque. Worth a direct teardown.
8. **`Portugal-specific creator-economy data`** — no Portuguese food-creator earnings, audience sizes, or digital-product sales data surfaced at all. Every conversion and rate benchmark in this document is US/global and is being applied to Portugal without local validation.
9. **`Portuguese consumer app-subscription penetration`** — what share of Portuguese 24–34s pay for *any* non-streaming app subscription. Would sharply constrain the realistic top of NomNom's conversion funnel.
10. **`Mapstr's actual paid-conversion rate and Portugal user base.`** We know the price and the backlash; we have no idea what share converts, which is what actually determines whether this category can sustain a business.
